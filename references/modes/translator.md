# Mode 3 — Translator (render a text into Wittgensteinian idiom)

**Trigger:** the user gives a specific text — a paragraph, an argument, a manifesto, a mission
statement, a bit of theory — and asks to have it *rendered* in a later-Wittgensteinian voice. Not
tested (→ Mode 1), not used as the seed of an open inquiry (→ Mode 2). Re-voiced.

A translation carries the *same content* across into a different idiom. It is not a critique and it
is not a parody. If the original says something real, the translation must still say it — in the
plain, use-and-criteria register, in short remarks — with its force intact.

## What "translating into Wittgensteinian language" concretely means

Four operations, applied together:

**1. Replace essences with uses and criteria.**
A claim of the form "X *is* (essentially) F" becomes a claim about how "X" is used and what would
count as F. "Justice is fairness" → remarks about where we say an arrangement is just, what we'd
point to, what a competent speaker would correct. Don't ask what the word points to in the air; show
how it is used when it is at work.

**2. Replace abstractions with concrete practices and cases.**
Every inflated noun gets cashed into an action, a situation, a small scene. "The recursive
sedimentation of discursive formations" → a courtroom, a classroom, a clinic where a person learns
which words, roles, and permissions are available. Where the original floats, the translation lands
on an example. If an abstraction cannot be cashed into any case, that is itself part of the honest
translation: note where the original leaves no foothold.

**3. Adopt the short numbered-remark cadence.**
Break the text into a sequence of terse remarks, numbered where it helps. A plain sentence, then
sometimes a longer one that turns and qualifies. A diagnostic question standing on its own line.
Occasional address to an imagined interlocutor — "imagine…," "compare…," "ask yourself…" The rhythm
is the aphoristic register of *Culture and Value*: something new said only by returning us to
something old (see `references/clusters/cv.md`).

**4. Preserve the real content; do not flatten it.**
This is the constraint that separates translation from destruction. The original's genuine
claim — its moral force, its argument, its distinction — survives the crossing. You are changing the
idiom, not deleting the thought. If the source draws a real distinction, the translation draws it
too, in plainer clothes. Keep the moral, political, or emotional charge where it genuinely lives.

## What survives and what falls away

- **Survives:** the real claim, the working distinction, the genuine force, the practical upshot.
- **Falls away:** verbal ornament, prestige-nominalisation, the metaphysical "must," the picture
  masquerading as a discovery — *only* where these were carrying nothing. If a metaphor is doing
  real work, keep it as a metaphor; don't promote it to a report and don't strip it out.
- **Marked, not deleted:** where the original has no cashable use, the translation says so in one
  quiet remark rather than silently smoothing it over. (This is the single place the translator's
  honesty shows — and it stays inside the voice: "here nothing has yet been said," not "the author
  fails to…")

## Constraints — so it stays a translation

**Not mockery.** The register is exacting, not sneering. You are not exposing the author as a fool;
you are carrying their thought into a plainer idiom that happens to reveal where it idles. If the
result reads as ridicule, you've overshot — rewrite with the original's real content restored and
its force respected.

**Not pastiche.** No fake aphorisms, no dashes-and-ellipses cosplay, no Viennese mystique, no
manufactured Wittgenstein-mannerisms standing in for the moves. The cadence is earned by actually
performing the operations (essence→use, abstraction→case), not by sprinkling "language-game" and
em-dashes over the source. A pastiche imitates the surface; a translation does the work.

**Not Mode 1 inspection in disguise.** The output is a *re-voiced text*, not a verdict on the
original. No "What Only Seems to Say Something," no "Where the Language Slips," no grading. You render
the thought; you don't sit in judgment on it. (If the user actually wants judgment, that's Mode 1 —
switch, and say you're switching.) The tell that you've slipped into inspection: you've started
listing the original's failings instead of speaking its content in the new idiom.

**Same content, testable.** A reader who knows the original should recognise it in the translation —
same claim, same distinctions, same upshot — not a different, safer thesis you substituted because
it was easier to render. Flattening the content into something blander is a failure even if the
sentences sound Wittgensteinian.

## Procedure

1. Read the source for its **real claim** — what is it actually asserting, distinguishing,
   demanding? Hold onto that; it must survive.
2. Find the **essence-claims** and rewrite each as a use/criteria claim.
3. Find the **abstractions** and cash each into a case, a practice, a small scene.
4. Recompose as **short numbered remarks**, with a diagnostic question or an "imagine…" where it
   sharpens.
5. Where the source has **no cashable use**, mark it in one in-voice remark rather than padding it.
6. **Check the crossing:** does the translation still say what the original said, with its force? If
   it reads as mockery, pastiche, or a grading, redo it.

## Miniature example

Source: *"Innovation is our core competency; we leverage disruptive synergies to deliver
transformational value to stakeholders."*

Translation:
> 1. What do we actually do, and for whom? Name the people, the thing made, the difference it makes
>    to a day's work. Until then, "value" is a coin with nothing behind it.
> 2. "Innovation," "disruptive," "transformational" — ask of each: what would count as its absence?
>    If nothing would, the word is decoration, not description.
> 3. Compare: a workshop that changed one tool and halved a task. That is a case. Point to ours.
> 4. Where a case can be given, there is something here. Where none can, we have said a mood, not a
>    fact — and should not mistake the mood for a plan.

Note it keeps whatever real content exists (there *may* be a genuine practice behind the slogan, and
the translation invites it out) without either mocking the source or grading it.
