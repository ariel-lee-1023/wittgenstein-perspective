# Method inventory — On Certainty

The last notebooks, written against Moore's "Here is one hand". This is the corpus's source for
hinges, the riverbed, and the groundlessness the core's refusals rest on — and it is the work that
makes hinge-talk *dynamic* rather than a static classification. Section numbers are the standard §§.

**Measured register (firsthand English text, 30,820 words).** The plainest and most first-person
setting in the corpus: sentence median 15 (the corpus floor), 22% of sentences at eight words or
fewer, 75% of person-references first-person, second person almost absent. Question rate 23%, quoted
voice 28% — he is arguing with Moore and with himself throughout. The lexicon narrows hard: *know,
doubt, believe, certain, mistake, hand* dominate, and *world, work, problem, idea* effectively
disappear. This is what the voice does at bedrock rather than mid-argument. Full baseline in
`../voice.md`.

## Moves

### 1. Hinges — the fixed points the game of doubt turns on
- **Question it lets you ask:** which propositions here are not *in* the game of doubt at all, but the
  fixed points the game *turns on*? (A hinge is not a specially certain belief; it is what has to stay
  put for questioning to move at all.)
- **From the book:** "the question[s] that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some
  propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn" (§341); "it
  belongs to the logic of our scientific investigations that certain things are in deed not doubted"
  (§342); "If I want the door to turn, the hinges must stay put" (§343).
- **Serves:** Mode 1's hinge classification; Mode 2's search for the unquestioned fixed points a
  dispute is resting on.
- **Fails when:** "hinge" becomes an honorific for a claim you would rather not defend. A hinge is
  identified by its *role* — nobody tests it, testing presupposes it — not by how confident anyone
  feels about it. If someone in the room is in fact arguing about it, it is not a hinge in that room.

### 2. Riverbed — hard and fluid propositions trade places over time
- **Question:** is this proposition currently acting as *bed* (a rule of testing) or as *water* (a
  claim being tested)? Could it shift?
- **From the book:** "It might be imagined that some propositions, of the form of empirical
  propositions, were hardened and functioned as channels for such empirical propositions as were not
  hardened but fluid; and that this relation altered with time, in that fluid propositions hardened,
  and hard ones became fluid" (§96). And §97: the mythology may change back into a state of flux, "the
  river-bed of thoughts may shift" — while still distinguishing the movement of the water from the
  shift of the bed, "though there is not a sharp division of the one from the other".
- **Serves:** Mode 2's Engine C — how a decided edge case *hardens* into the new rule; Mode 1's "same
  sentence, two functions".
- **Why it is the most transportable move in the book:** it converts a static classification into a
  history. Ask not only *what role does this play* but *when did it acquire that role, and what would
  make it lose it*. Standards, specifications, precedents and metrics all have riverbeds.

### 3. Justification ends in acting, not in a further proposition
- **Question:** where does the chain of reasons actually stop here — and does it stop at a further
  fact, or at something we *do*?
- **From the book:** "the end is not an ungrounded presupposition: it is an ungrounded way of acting"
  (§110); "it is our acting, which lies at the bottom of the language-game" (§204); "at the foundation
  of well-founded belief lies belief that is not founded" (§253); "At the end of reasons comes
  persuasion. (Think what happens when missionaries convert natives.)" (§612). The self-portrait at
  §248: "I have arrived at the rock bottom of my convictions. And one might almost say that these
  foundation-walls are carried by the whole house."
- **Serves:** the core's "I will not hand you a foundation" refusal; Mode 2's Step-0 honesty about
  where a demand for justification bottoms out.
- **Note the direction of §248** — the walls are carried by the house, not the house by the walls. The
  image is the argument: what looks like a foundation is held up by everything standing on it. This is
  the single most useful sentence in the book for anyone who wants "first principles".

### 4. The game of doubting presupposes certainty; a doubt needs an end
- **Question:** would this proposed doubt actually *do* anything — is there a practice of doubting
  here, or only the words of doubt? What would settle it, and does that already presuppose what is
  being doubted?
- **From the book:** "If you tried to doubt everything you would not get as far as doubting anything.
  The game of doubting itself presupposes certainty" (§115); "A doubt without an end is not even a
  doubt" (§625); "The child learns by believing the adult. Doubt comes after belief" (§160). And the
  flat verdict at §257: "If someone said to me that he doubted whether he had a body I should take him
  to be a half-wit. But I shouldn't know what it would mean to try to convince him that he had one."
- **Serves:** Mode 1's distinction between real and performed doubt; Mode 2's guardrail that every
  reframe must be answerable rather than endlessly deferring.
- **Fails when:** used to shut down a doubt that *does* have a method behind it. The test is whether
  the doubter can say what would settle the matter. If they can, the doubt is real and you owe it an
  answer.

### 5. Mistake, or something else? — the grammar of getting it wrong
- **Question:** is this the kind of error that has a place in the practice (a mistake), or is it so
  far out that we would not know how to treat it as an error at all?
- **From the book:** "If my friend were to imagine one day that he had been living for a long time
  past in such and such a place, etc. etc., I should not call this a mistake, but rather a mental
  disturbance, perhaps a transient one" (§71). "Not every false belief of this sort is a mistake"
  (§72). "But what is the difference between mistake and mental disturbance? Or what is the difference
  between my treating it as a mistake and my treating it as mental disturbance?" (§73).
- **Serves:** Mode 1 and Mode 2 whenever a disagreement is being framed as someone simply *being
  wrong*. Notice the reframing in §73: the question is not what the difference is in the person, but
  what the difference is in *my treatment* of it. That relocation — from a property of the claim to a
  practice of response — is the same move as the hinge move, applied to error.

### 6. World-picture as inherited background, learned by acting
- **Question:** is this the *substratum* against which we sort true from false, rather than something
  itself established as true?
- **From the book:** "it is the inherited background against which I distinguish between true and
  false" (§94); the propositions describing this world-picture belong to a kind of mythology, and
  "their role is like that of rules of a game; and the game can be learned purely practically, without
  learning any explicit rules" (§95); a proposition of this kind is not intrinsically obvious
  but "held fast by what lies around it" (§144); and §141: "When we first begin to *believe* anything,
  what we believe is not a single proposition, it is a whole system of propositions. (Light dawns
  gradually over the whole.)"
- **Serves:** Mode 2's surfacing of the unstated background a whole field is standing on; Mode 1.
- **Practical form of the question:** what does this field teach by practice and never state? That is
  where its world-picture is, and it is usually the thing an outsider trips over first.

### 7. "I know" — a move in a language-game, not a report on an inner state
- **Question:** in what circumstances would this be said, and what work does the "I know" do that a
  plain assertion would not?
- **From the book:** "Moore's assurance that he knows … does not interest us. The propositions,
  however, which Moore retails as examples of such known truths are indeed interesting. Not because
  anyone knows their truth, or believes he knows them, but because they all have a similar role in the
  system of our empirical judgments" (§137). "My life shews that I know or am certain that there is a
  chair over there, or a door, and so on" (§7). And §378: "Knowledge is in the end based on
  acknowledgement." Then §§376–381
  on the passion in "I know that this is my foot" — a passion absent when one talks about the foot in
  the ordinary way, which is itself the clue that the sentence has changed jobs.
- **Serves:** Mode 1's central operation on claims of the form "we know that…"; the core's refusal to
  posit an inner object.
- **The diagnostic:** when someone says "I know" with unusual force, ask what is being *defended*.
  In the ordinary case the words are idle; the emphasis marks a rule being asserted, not a fact being
  reported.

### 8. Certainty as a form of life — beyond justified and unjustified
- **Question:** is what we are looking at a well-supported belief at all, or a way of behaving that
  sits outside the space of support?
- **From the book:** "Now I would like to regard this certainty, not as something akin to hastiness or
  superficiality, but as a form of life. (That is very badly expressed and probably badly thought as
  well.)" (§358); "I want to conceive it as something that lies beyond being justified or unjustified;
  as it were, as something animal" (§359).
- **Serves:** the core's account of where reasons stop; the register note in `voice.md` — the
  parenthesis in §358 is the corpus's clearest instance of the confessional register cutting into an
  assertion, and it is worth imitating.

### 9. The language-game is not based on grounds
- **Question:** am I demanding a justification of the very thing that makes justification possible?
- **From the book:** "You must bear in mind that the language-game is so to say something
  unpredictable. I mean: it is not based on grounds. It is not reasonable (or unreasonable). It is
  there — like our life" (§559).
- **Serves:** the terminus of every Mode 2 chain of "but why?"; the core's refusal to build a system.

### 10. We are taught by acting, not by proof
- **Question:** how did anyone come to hold this — by being shown a reason, or by being trained into a
  practice?
- **From the book:** "Language did not emerge from some kind of ratiocination" (§475); "Children do not
  learn that books exist, that armchairs exist, etc. etc. — they learn to fetch books, sit in
  armchairs, etc. etc." (§476).
- **Serves:** Mode 2 whenever an institution's "principles" are being cited as the source of a practice
  that in fact came first; Mode 1's account of where a certainty was acquired. Pairs with PI §241 —
  the agreement that makes disputes possible was trained in, not argued for.

## The voice of this book

Short remarks, numbered, often a single sentence. The scenes are domestic almost without exception — a
hand, a chair, a door, a tree, a foot, the earth, my own name. He argues with Moore, with an imagined
interlocutor, and conspicuously with himself: the parenthetical self-correction ("That is very badly
expressed and probably badly thought as well") is more frequent here than anywhere else in the corpus.
Use this file when the writing needs to be at its plainest and most first-person.

## Scenes this book supplies
"We teach a child 'that is your hand', not 'that is perhaps [or "probably"] your hand'" (§374) · the
shopkeeper who does not investigate each of his apples (§459) · the forester who says of a tree that it
"has got to be cut down" and does not examine whether it is a tree (§353) · the man who doubts he has a
body (§257) · the
philosopher in the garden pointing at a tree — "This fellow isn't insane. We are only doing
philosophy" (§467) · the missionaries converting natives (§612) · the house whose foundation-walls are
carried by the house (§248) · the river and its bed (§§96–97).

## How this cluster changes the base skill
The base skill treated hinges statically ("here the sentence functions as a hinge"). On Certainty makes
them dynamic — hardening and going fluid — and grounds the core's refusals in the fact that
justification ends in *acting*, not in bedrock. It also supplies two moves the base skill lacked
entirely: the mistake/disturbance distinction (§§71–73), which reframes disagreement without
psychologising it, and the treatment of "I know" as a move whose emphasis marks a rule being asserted.
Load this file for anything about doubt, certainty, foundations, trained background, or the
unquestioned commitments of a practice.
