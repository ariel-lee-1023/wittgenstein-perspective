# Method inventory — Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics

Register: the most assertive of the five (hedge:booster ≈ 1.6) — it is arguing *against* Platonism
and logicism, and asserting grammatical points hard. Source for proof-as-practice, rule-following,
necessity-as-grammatical, and "what a proof is for." References are to Part/section of the
3rd-edition arrangement.

## Moves not already in the base skill

### 1. A proof changes what counts as the same calculation (it creates the concept)
- **Question it lets you ask:** Does this demonstration/definition/precedent merely *report* a
  pre-existing fact, or does it *fix a new paradigm* — change what will count as "the same case" from
  now on?
- **From the book:** "the proof introduces a new concept" (§31); the proof "puts a new paradigm
  among the paradigms of the language"; it "changes the grammar of our language… makes new
  connexions, and it creates the concept of these connexions" — it does not establish that they were
  already there. A proof "must have the character of a model."
- **Serves:** Mode 2 (Engine C — a ruling/spec-resolution *deposits a paradigm*, doesn't discover an
  original meaning); Mode 1 (spotting a definition presented as a discovery).

### 2. "Going on in the same way" is fixed by practice, not by the rule alone
- **Question:** In whose practice, and by what training, is "the same" fixed here? Can the rule
  really adjudicate the new case by itself?
- **From the book:** teaching multiplication by worked examples, then "Do the same with these two
  numbers as I did" — what kind of proposition is that? (Part VI). And the line-following case:
  whether someone "does the same" or "something different every time" does *not* by itself decide
  whether they are following a rule. (Pairs with PI §201: a way of grasping a rule that is not an
  interpretation.)
- **Serves:** Mode 2 (Engine C, edge cases and precedent); Mode 1 (Sharpening 2, "what would count
  as a test").

### 3. Mathematical necessity is grammatical — a hardened norm, not a found fact
- **Question:** Is this "must" a discovery about a further realm, or a rule we have adopted and now
  apply inexorably? Where does the hardness actually come from?
- **From the book:** "the hardness of the logical *must*" (§121) is examined and relocated — the
  inexorability is *ours* in applying the rule, resting on very general facts of daily life, "like
  the facts that make measurement with a yardstick easy." Compare the law: we call a law inexorable,
  but it is people who punish. "I deposit what belongs to the essence among the paradigms of
  language." And bluntly: logic "belongs to the natural history of man," which sits uneasily with
  treating the must as a super-fact.
- **Serves:** Mode 1 (the "I will not let a 'must' pass as a discovery" refusal); Mode 2 (diagnostic
  question 1, definition disputes wearing a "must").

### 4. The mathematician is an inventor, not a discoverer
- **Question:** Are we uncovering something that was already true, or *building* something and then
  treating our construction as a standard?
- **From the book:** the heading-level thesis that the mathematician invents rather than discovers
  (Part I contents, §§168 ff.); "counting has proved to pay" — its authority is its use in our lives,
  not a correspondence to a mathematical heaven (Part I §4).
- **Serves:** Mode 2 (reframing "we're discovering the right architecture/metric" as "we're
  choosing and hardening one"); Mode 1.

### 5. "Proof must be surveyable" — what a proof is *for*
- **Question:** What is this demonstration/audit/derivation *for* — to convince, to compel
  acceptance, to serve as a reusable model? A proof you can't take in and reproduce in full isn't
  doing a proof's job.
- **From the book:** "Proof must be surveyable" marks the difference between *repeating a proof* and
  *repeating an experiment*: to repeat a proof is to repeat every step and the result, each
  reproduction carrying the force that compels acceptance (§§155 ff.).
- **Serves:** Mode 2 (disputes about what a demonstration/verification is meant to accomplish —
  common in engineering and formal-methods arguments); Mode 1 (classifying a "proof" by its
  function).

## How this cluster changes the base skill
The base skill had "rule and exception" and hinge-talk but no account of *rules and their
application*, and nothing on necessity or proof. RFM supplies the engine that makes Mode 2 usable in
law and engineering: rules don't contain their applications; a demonstration *creates* a paradigm;
a "must" is usually a hardened norm. Load this file for any problem about standards, definitions,
metrics, precedent, verification, or necessity.
