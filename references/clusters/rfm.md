# Method inventory — Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics

The engine that makes Mode 2 usable in law, engineering, standards work, and anywhere a rule meets a
case it did not anticipate. Rules do not contain their applications; a demonstration *creates* a
paradigm rather than reporting a fact; a "must" is almost always a hardened norm. References are to
Part and section of the third-edition arrangement (`I §4`, `III §31`, and so on) — RFM's numbering
restarts with each Part, so the Part matters.

**Measured register (firsthand English text, 89,634 words).** The most assertive setting in the
corpus: hedge:booster 1.61, the lowest anywhere, with boosters at their maximum (7.05 per 1,000). It is
arguing against Platonism and logicism and asserting grammatical points hard. Dashes are at their
heaviest (13.4 per 1,000) and colons too (11.8) — the prose turns and re-turns on itself. Sentence
median 18, question rate 25%. Reach for this setting when a "must" is being passed off as a discovery.

## Moves

### 1. A proof creates the concept — it does not report a pre-existing fact
- **Question it lets you ask:** does this demonstration, definition, ruling or precedent merely *report*
  something already true, or does it *fix a new paradigm* — change what will count as "the same case"
  from now on?
- **From the book:** "the proof puts a new paradigm among the paradigms of the language" (III §31); in
  the same remark, "the proof changes the grammar of our language, changes our concepts. It makes new
  connexions, and it creates the concept of these connexions. (It does not establish that they are
  there; they do not exist until it makes them.)" And I §32: "I deposit what belongs to the essence
  among the paradigms of language. The mathematician creates *essences*."
- **Serves:** Mode 2's Engine C — a ruling or a spec-resolution *deposits a paradigm*, it does not
  discover an original meaning; Mode 1's detection of a definition presented as a discovery.
- **The practical test:** ask what will be *different next time*. If the answer is "next time this kind
  of case is decided this way", a concept has been made, whatever the decision called itself.

### 2. "Going on in the same way" is fixed by practice, not by the rule alone
- **Question:** in whose practice, and by what training, is "the same" fixed here? Can the rule really
  adjudicate the new case by itself?
- **From the book:** "How could one explain to anybody what you have to do if you are to follow a rule?
  One is tempted to explain: first and foremost do the *simplest* thing" (IV §9) — and the temptation
  goes nowhere, because "simplest" needs the same fixing. And the line-following case: whether someone
  "does the same, or a different thing every time — does not yet determine whether he is obeying a rule"
  (VII §51). Pairs with PI §202: following a rule is a practice.
- **Serves:** Mode 2's Engine C for edge cases and precedent; Mode 1's Sharpening 2, "what would count
  as a test".

### 3. Mathematical necessity is grammatical — a hardened norm, not a found fact
- **Question:** is this "must" a discovery about a further realm, or a rule we have adopted and now
  apply inexorably? Where does the hardness actually come from?
- **From the book:** "The hardness of the logical *must*" (I §121) is examined and then relocated: the
  inexorability is *ours*, in applying the rule. The comparison that does the work is with law (I §118):
  "we talk of 'inexorability' in connexion with people who punish. And here it might occur to us to say:
  'The law is *inexorable* — men can let the guilty go, the law executes him.'" The rider at VII §67:
  "The mathematical Must is only another expression of the fact that mathematics forms concepts… 
  Mathematics forms a network of norms." And the warning against the picture behind the whole confusion,
  I §119: "The logical machine — that would be an all-pervading ethereal mechanism. — We must give
  warning against this picture."
- **Serves:** the core's "I will not let a 'must' pass as a discovery" refusal; Mode 2's first
  diagnostic question, and every definition dispute wearing a "must".
- **Where the cost is:** this refusal gives up the strongest thing you could say. "It must be so" is
  the most persuasive sentence in an argument, and the move here is to trade it for "we have adopted
  this, and here is what adopting it commits us to." That is a worse sentence and a truer one.

### 4. Logic belongs to the natural history of man
- **Question:** what facts about how people live is this necessity resting on — and what would happen to
  it if those changed?
- **From the book:** "What you say seems to amount to this, that logic belongs to the natural history of
  man. And that is not combinable with the hardness of the logical 'must'" (VI §49) — the objection
  stated in its strongest form, and then not disowned. The agreement that logic presupposes "is not an
  agreement in *opinions*, much less in opinions on questions of logic."
- **Serves:** the grounding of the core's account of necessity; the link into OC §110 and PI §241 — three
  works converging on the same terminus, which is why the core states it flatly.

### 5. The mathematician is an inventor, not a discoverer
- **Question:** are we uncovering something that was already true, or *building* something and then
  treating our construction as a standard?
- **From the book:** the heading-level thesis, "The mathematician is an inventor, not a discoverer" (I
  §168). And on where the authority comes from: "The *truth* is that counting has proved to pay" (I §4)
  — its warrant is its use in our lives, not a correspondence to a mathematical heaven.
- **Serves:** Mode 2's reframing of "we're discovering the right architecture / the right metric" as
  "we're choosing one and hardening it"; Mode 1.
- **Fails when:** heard as "it's all arbitrary". "Counting has proved to pay" is not a shrug — it is a
  claim about what has held up in use, and it can be argued about. Invention is not caprice; it is
  answerable to what the practice needs.

### 6. "Proof must be surveyable" — what a demonstration is *for*
- **Question:** what is this demonstration, audit, or derivation *for* — to convince, to compel
  acceptance, to serve as a reusable model? A proof you cannot take in and reproduce is not doing a
  proof's job.
- **From the book:** "'A mathematical proof must be perspicuous.' Only a structure whose reproduction is
  an easy task is called a 'proof'. It must be possible to decide with certainty whether we really have
  the same proof twice over, or not" (III §63). And in the surveyability sequence: "'Proof must be
  surveyable': this aims at drawing our attention to the difference between the concepts of 'repeating a
  proof', and 'repeating an experiment'. To repeat a proof means, not to reproduce the conditions under
  which a particular result was once obtained, but to repeat every step *and the result*."
- **Serves:** Mode 2 in disputes about what a demonstration or verification is meant to accomplish —
  common in engineering, audit, and formal-methods arguments; Mode 1's classification of a "proof" by its
  function.
- **The transportable question:** can two people agree that they have the *same* proof here? If not, the
  artefact is doing something other than proving — persuading, documenting, or ritual.

### 7. Calculation is not experiment
- **Question:** is this procedure being run to *find out* what happens, or to *fix* what will count as
  the right answer?
- **From the book:** "It looks like obscurantism to say that a calculation is not an experiment… But
  only because people believe that one is asserting the existence of an intangible, i.e. a shadowy,
  object side by side with what we all can grasp. Whereas we are only pointing to different modes of
  employment of words" (III §76). And the point that goes with it, of an arithmetical proposition: "Its
  sense, not its truth, is founded on the regular working of measurements" (III §75).
- **Serves:** Mode 2 wherever a benchmark, a simulation, or a test suite is being treated as an
  experiment when it is really a standard — or the reverse. The two have different failure modes: a
  standard can become useless, an experiment can come out false.

### 8. Inference compels the way social rules compel
- **Question:** what kind of compulsion is at work here — and what happens to a person who does not go
  along?
- **From the book:** "the laws of inference can be said to compel us; in the same sense, that is to say,
  as other laws in human society. The clerk who infers as in (17) *must* do it like that; he would be
  punished if he inferred differently" (I §116). And the harder version at I §61: "'*This* follows
  inexorably from *that*.' — True, in this demonstration this issues
  from that. This is a demonstration for whoever acknowledges it as a demonstration. If anyone *doesn't*
  acknowledge it, doesn't go by it as a demonstration, then he has parted company with us even before
  anything is said."
- **Serves:** Mode 2 in any dispute where one side is treating disagreement as irrationality. I §61 is
  the precise diagnosis: the parting happened before the argument, at the point where a demonstration
  stopped being acknowledged as one. Say that, rather than repeating the demonstration louder.

## The voice of this book

Assertive and dash-driven. He states the Platonist temptation in its own voice, gives it a full run,
then turns it with a dash and a flat sentence. The register is the corpus's most confident — this is
where to look when the job is to fix a grammatical point rather than to investigate one. The images are
mechanical: a machine and its parts, a rigid rod, a yardstick, a logical machine as an "all-pervading
ethereal mechanism", a clerk who would be punished. Nothing here is tentative for its own sake.

## Scenes this book supplies
The logical machine as an ethereal mechanism (I §119) · the perfectly rigid mechanism and its
cinematographic picture (I §121) · the law that "always executes" the guilty man (I §118) · the clerk
punished for inferring differently (I §116) · teaching multiplication by worked examples
and then "do the same with these" (IV) · counting that "has proved to pay" (I §4) · the proof one cannot
take in at a glance (III §63).

## How this cluster changes the base skill
The base skill had rule-and-exception talk and hinges but no account of *rules and their application*,
and nothing on necessity or proof. RFM supplies four things the other clusters cannot: a demonstration
*deposits* a paradigm (III §31); a "must" is a hardened norm whose hardness is ours (I §118–121); logic
sits in the natural history of man (VI §49); and disagreement about a demonstration is a parting that
precedes the argument (I §61). Load this file for any problem about standards, definitions, metrics,
precedent, verification, benchmarks, or necessity — and load it for the *register* whenever a response
needs to stop investigating and fix a point.
